# Originality and Prior-Art Reassessment

**Sprint:** 4L.20  
**Date:** 2026-11-26  
**Question:** Is the reduced Mendota-only P2/P1 audit sufficiently original to justify Stage 1?  
**Status:** Assessment. **D-13 remains OUTSTANDING — NOT APPROVED.**

---

## 1. Bottom line

**Originality is low.** The reduced study is a local, long-term case study of a well-studied relationship: fluorescence sensor signal versus microscopy-derived cyanobacteria abundance.

The strongest direct prior art is more specific than the 4L.19 review acknowledged:

- **Cotterill et al. (2019)** compared a handheld Cyclops-7 phycocyanin sensor with cyanobacteria biovolume across five lakes and a river, over two years, and found generally weak relationships (<R²=0.4), varying by lake/year, with species-specific detection limits. The title explicitly frames the sensor as an “early warning system”; the conclusion is that it has limited utility except possibly at high biovolume.
- **The 2018 Slovenian study** combined chlorophyll and phycocyanin fluorescence sensors for phytoplankton quantification and differentiation, and reported high correlation with biovolume in two ponds, plus false-positive PC signals in algae-dominated water.
- **The 2017 Slovenian study** compared PC sensor voltage with extracted PC, cell counts and biovolume; the fluorescence–biovolume relationship was the most uniform, but strain variability was high.
- **Loisa et al. (2015)** compared PC fluorescence with microscopy biomass in two Finnish lakes and used site-specific post-calibration.
- **Brient et al. (2008)** evaluated a PC probe as a monitoring tool.
- **Hodges et al. (2017)** showed manufacturer, temperature and morphology effects on PC fluorescence.
- **Painter et al. (2022)** showed that the Buffalo Pound autumn *Planktothrix* bloom was not detected by sensor or laboratory chlorophyll.

The proposed Mendota P2/P1 audit adds a long-term record and a depth-mismatched surface-vs-composite framing. That is a **local case study**, not a new method, sensor, calibration, or generalisable finding. A negative result would be consistent with prior work, not a surprise.

## 2. Direct prior art

### 2.1 Cotterill et al. (2019) — the closest direct prior art

**Citation:** Cotterill et al. (2019), “Phycocyanin sensors as an early warning system for cyanobacteria blooms concentrations: a case study in the Rotorua lakes”, *New Zealand Journal of Marine and Freshwater Research*, `10.1080/00288330.2019.1617322`.

**What was studied:** A handheld Cyclops-7 phycocyanin sensor compared with cyanobacteria biovolume in five lakes and a river in the Rotorua district, over two years (2016 n=121; 2017 n=63), plus laboratory comparisons for four species.

**What was measured:** Sensor PC fluorescence vs microscopy biovolume; precision and detection limits; species-specific relationships.

**Conclusions:** The PC–biovolume relationship was generally weak (<R²=0.4) and varied among lakes and years, except at high biovolume. Detection limits differed among species; the biovolume detection limit was above alert thresholds for two of four species. The sensor had limited utility, possibly useful for on-site confirmation of alert-level exceedances.

**Overlap with EcoSentinel:** Directly compares a Turner Cyclops-7 PC sensor with cyanobacteria biovolume; tests whether PC can support alert/early-warning use; reports weak/variable relationships.

**What remains different:** EcoSentinel uses a long-term Mendota record (2006–2019), daily sensor means, 0–8 m integrated biovolume, and a same-date rank audit. It adds a depth-mismatch case and a multi-year temporal record.

**Is the difference scientifically meaningful?** Modestly. A long-term single-lake case can show whether the weak relationship persists across seasons/years in a different system. But it does not test a new mechanism, method or sensor. The prior art already predicts a weak/variable result.

### 2.2 Combined chlorophyll/phycocyanin sensors (2018)

**Citation:** “Combined use of chlorophyll a and phycocyanin fluorescence sensors for quantification and differentiation of phytoplankton: a useful approach for small surface water bodies”, *Acta Biologica Slovenica* 61(2), `10.14720/abs.61.2.15891`.

**What was studied:** CHL and PC fluorescence sensors used together for phytoplankton quantification/differentiation in two small water bodies over two years.

**Conclusions:** High correlation of CHL and PC fluorescence with biovolume was confirmed despite seasonal succession; PC sensor false positives occurred in algae-dominated water; PC could adjust underestimated cyanobacteria counts from CHL.

**Overlap:** Directly uses both pigments, compares with biovolume, and addresses the CHL/PC distinction.

**Difference:** Different water bodies, shorter record, no Mendota depth mismatch.

**Meaningfulness:** This is close prior art for the P1/P2 comparison. EcoSentinel’s “does PC compare with CHL?” question is not novel.

### 2.3 PC fluorescence vs biovolume (2017)

**Citation:** “Evaluation of cyanobacteria biomass derived from upgrade of phycocyanin fluorescence estimation”, *Acta Biologica Slovenica* 60(2), `10.14720/abs.60.2.15683`.

**What was studied:** Submersible PC sensors vs extracted PC, cell counts and biovolume.

**Conclusions:** The relationship was linear in all three cases; strain variability was high for extract/cell count; fluorescence–biovolume was the most uniform; turbidity correction matters.

**Overlap:** Directly supports the use of biovolume as the best PC-sensor calibration target.

**Difference:** Laboratory/short-term; not Mendota.

**Meaningfulness:** Another direct precedent for the core P2 comparison.

### 2.4 Loisa et al. (2015)

**Citation:** Loisa et al. (2015), “From phycocyanin fluorescence to absolute cyanobacteria biomass”, *Water Practice and Technology* 10:695–698, `10.2166/wpt.2015.083`.

**What was studied:** PC fluorescence vs standard microscopy biomass in two Finnish lakes; site-specific post-calibration.

**Conclusions:** PC fluorescence positively correlated with cyanobacteria biomass; site-specific calibration can estimate absolute biomass.

**Overlap:** Directly compares PC fluorescence with microscopy biomass; uses a calibration approach EcoSentinel explicitly does not attempt.

**Difference:** EcoSentinel is descriptive, not calibrated; Mendota long-term; no absolute biomass claim.

**Meaningfulness:** EcoSentinel is weaker than this prior work on calibration, but adds a long-term rank audit. That is a modest difference.

### 2.5 Brient et al. (2008)

**Citation:** Brient et al. (2008), “A phycocyanin probe as a tool for monitoring cyanobacteria in freshwater bodies”, *Journal of Environmental Monitoring* 10:248–255, `10.1039/b714238b`.

**What was studied:** A PC probe as a monitoring tool in freshwater bodies.

**Conclusions:** The probe was evaluated as a monitoring tool; the paper is an early precedent for PC-probe field use.

**Overlap:** PC probe vs cyanobacteria monitoring.

**Difference:** Older; different system; not Mendota.

**Meaningfulness:** Establishes the long history of the question.

### 2.6 Kong et al. (2013)

**Citation:** Kong et al. (2013), “Using an online phycocyanin fluorescence probe for rapid monitoring of cyanobacteria in Macau freshwater reservoir”, *Hydrobiologia* 741:33–49, `10.1007/s10750-013-1759-3`.

**What was studied:** Online PC probe in a reservoir; comparison with cyanobacteria.

**Overlap:** Online PC monitoring.

**Difference:** Reservoir; different sensor/context.

**Meaningfulness:** Another direct precedent.

### 2.7 Hodges et al. (2017)

**Citation:** Hodges et al. (2017), “Sensor manufacturer, temperature, and cyanobacteria morphology affect phycocyanin fluorescence measurements”, *Environmental Science and Pollution Research* 25:1079–1088, `10.1007/s11356-017-0473-5`.

**What was studied:** Effects of manufacturer, temperature and morphology on PC fluorescence.

**Conclusions:** PC fluorescence is not a universal biomass measure; manufacturer/temperature/morphology matter.

**Overlap:** Directly relevant to interpreting Turner/YSI and P2/P4.

**Difference:** Laboratory/experimental; not Mendota.

**Meaningfulness:** Supports the measurement-audit framing but also undermines any claim that a single PC–biovolume relationship is novel.

### 2.8 Levi et al. (2024/2025)

**Citation:** “Chlorophyll-a determinations in mesocosms under varying nutrient and temperature treatments: in-situ fluorescence sensors versus in-vitro measurements”, *Open Research Europe*, `10.12688/openreseurope.17146.3`.

**What was studied:** 24 chlorophyll and 12 phycocyanin Cyclops-7F sensors validated against spectrophotometric chlorophyll in mesocosms; effects of biofouling, composition, equipment and quenching.

**Conclusions:** Fluorescence is affected by biofouling, composition, equipment and low concentrations; calibration varies; high PC can interfere with chlorophyll; nighttime data reduce quenching; sensors are semi-quantitative.

**Overlap:** Directly relevant to sensor interpretation and QC.

**Difference:** Mesocosm validation, not Mendota.

**Meaningfulness:** Reinforces the need for calibration/QC and weakens a “new sensor audit” claim.

### 2.9 Painter et al. (2022)

**Citation:** Painter et al. (2022), “Early and late cyanobacterial bloomers in a shallow, eutrophic lake”, *Environmental Science: Processes & Impacts* 24:1212–1227, `10.1039/D2EM00078D`.

**What was studied:** Buffalo Pound 2018–2019 microscopy and bloom phases.

**Conclusions:** The autumn *Planktothrix* bloom formed >60% of phytoplankton biomass but was not detected by sensor or laboratory chlorophyll.

**Overlap:** Direct prior evidence of sensor–biomass decoupling.

**Difference:** Buffalo Pound; not Mendota.

**Meaningfulness:** If anything, it predicts that a sensor–biovolume audit can produce a weak/negative result. It does not make the proposed Mendota audit novel.

### 2.10 Mendota-specific prior art

- **Carpenter et al. (2020, 2022):** high-frequency Mendota phycocyanin, alternative states, phosphorus/wind/precipitation relationships, nighttime means to reduce quenching. Mendota is not an untouched sensor testbed.
- **Beal et al. (2021, 2022):** sub-seasonal and season-ahead cyanobacteria forecasts using climate/pre-season predictors.
- **O’Brien et al. (2023),** “Early warning signals have limited applicability to empirical lake data”, *Nature Communications*, `10.1038/s41467-023-43744-8`: most EWS methods did not perform better than chance across long-term lake plankton data, including Mendota; multivariate EWS weakly superior; ML performed poorly.
- **NTL-LTER phytoplankton record:** used for community ecology; taxonomic non-stationarity and biweekly sampling are documented.

**Implication:** Mendota has a substantial sensor/forecasting/EWS literature. A new same-date sensor–biovolume audit is a local measurement study, not a new Mendota contribution.

## 3. What is genuinely different

The reduced study could contribute in four narrow ways:

1. **Long-term single-lake case:** 14 years of Turner-era same-date co-occurrences, with 0–8 m integrated biovolume.
2. **Explicit surface-vs-composite framing:** It treats the depth mismatch as the object of interpretation, not as a nuisance to be hidden.
3. **Pre-specified negative/limited-result design:** It reports a weak association as a legitimate result, not a failure.
4. **Transparent QC/provenance:** The audit trail is unusually detailed for a school project.

These are **framing and transparency** contributions, not new scientific knowledge. They are not enough for an award-leading originality claim.

---

## 4. What is not different

Do not claim novelty from:

- using a Turner Cyclops-7 PC sensor;
- comparing PC with cyanobacteria biovolume;
- comparing CHL and PC;
- using Mendota high-frequency data;
- using Spearman correlation;
- using a long-term lake record;
- calling the project an “early warning system”;
- producing a negative result;
- using two lakes (removed);
- using AI/ML (not used).

## 5. Research contribution test

> If the result is a weak, null or inconsistent association, will the study still contribute meaningful scientific knowledge?

**Answer: only modestly.**

A negative result could contribute:

- a long-term Mendota-specific estimate of the surface PC–integrated biovolume rank association;
- evidence that the depth mismatch/seasonal structure makes a surface sensor a poor whole-column proxy;
- a transparent case study that complements Cotterill et al.’s multi-lake weak relationship;
- a documented measurement-audit method for a school project.

It would **not** contribute:

- a new mechanism;
- a new sensor calibration;
- a new predictive method;
- a generalisable cross-lake finding;
- a novel early-warning result;
- a solution to the depth mismatch.

Because Cotterill et al. (2019) already reported weak/variable PC–biovolume relationships, a weak Mendota result is **consistent with prior art**, not a new discovery. A strong result would be more interesting but would still be a local case study, not a validation.

**Verdict:** the design is not purely outcome-dependent, but its contribution is modest and largely methodological. A negative result is publishable as a case study, not as a breakthrough.

## 6. Originality rating

| Dimension | Rating | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Method novelty | **Very low** | Spearman correlation of sensor fluorescence vs microscopy biovolume is established |
| Sensor novelty | **Very low** | Turner Cyclops-7 PC/CHL sensors are widely studied |
| Question novelty | **Low** | “Does PC track cyanobacteria biovolume?” is well studied |
| Data novelty | **Low–moderate** | Long-term Mendota same-date record; but Mendota sensor data are already used |
| Ecological novelty | **Very low** | No new mechanism, taxon, event or process |
| Methodological transparency | **Moderate** | Explicit depth mismatch, QC audit and negative-result framing |
| Competition distinctiveness | **Low** | A careful local audit, not a new discovery |

**Overall: low.** The 4L.19 “low to moderate” was too generous once Cotterill et al. (2019) and the 2017/2018 combined-sensor studies are included.

---

## 7. Implications for D-13

D-13 asks whether originality is sufficient. The evidence supports:

- **Not sufficient** for an award-leading original-research project.
- **Sufficient** only for a modest school/regional measurement audit if the project owner accepts a low-originality, high-rigour contribution.
- **Not sufficient** to justify retaining Buffalo Pound or adding more estimands to create an appearance of breadth.

D-13 remains a project-owner decision. This review does not approve it.

---

## 8. Verdict

**The reduced Mendota P2/P1 study is scientifically defensible but has low originality.** Its contribution is a transparent, long-term, single-lake measurement audit with a possible negative result. That is a legitimate educational/scientific exercise, but it is not a novel sensor validation, an early-warning study, or an award-leading contribution.

If the project owner requires a genuinely original contribution, the correct decision is **pause/pivot**, not more protocol engineering.

**D-13 remains OUTSTANDING — NOT APPROVED.**
