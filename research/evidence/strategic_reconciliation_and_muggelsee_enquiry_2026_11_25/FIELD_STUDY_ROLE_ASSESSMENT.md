# Field Study Role Assessment

**Date:** 2026-11-25  
**Subject:** The student-led phycocyanin–biovolume field study described as the Sprint 4L.15 proposal.  
**Status:** Assessment only. No field study was designed, funded, permitted, or started.

---

## 1. Provenance

No Sprint 4L.15 package exists in the repository. The field-study proposal is therefore treated as an **externally described proposal**, not verified project state. This assessment evaluates its substance; it does not attribute it to a repository document that does not exist.

---

## 2. What the field study would test

The described question is:

> How do cyanobacterial community composition and environmental conditions influence the relationship between phycocyanin fluorescence and microscopy-derived biovolume?

That is a **measurement/calibration question**. It is not an early-warning question. It could produce a local species-specific calibration and evidence about interference, but it would not test bloom onset, lead time, or false-alarm rates.

---

## 3. Scientific value

### Strengths

- **Direct paired sampling.** It would measure sensor fluorescence and microscopy biovolume on the same water sample, which the current datasets cannot do.
- **Community composition.** It could show how species, colony size, pigment content, and morphology change the fluorescence-per-biovolume relationship.
- **Student ownership.** Field and laboratory work are visibly the student’s own.
- **Local relevance.** A local calibration could be useful for a lake or monitoring programme.

### Weaknesses and prior art

The core question is already well studied:

- Bastien et al. (2011) evaluated phycocyanin probes and highlighted species/cell-volume effects.
- Bastien et al. (2019) quantified species-specific measurement accuracy in culture.
- Cotterill et al. (2019) found weak and variable phycocyanin–biovolume relationships across lakes and years, except at high biovolume.
- Thomson-Laing et al. (2020) found strong culture relationships but lake- and species-specific fluorescence per biovolume.
- Ma et al. (2022) showed natural organic matter, temperature, and dominant species effects on online PC readings.
- Painter et al. (2022) already paired microscopy biomass and chlorophyll in Buffalo Pound and showed taxon/phase-dependent relationships, including a *Planktothrix* phase poorly tracked by chlorophyll.

A one-season field study would add a local case, not a new general mechanism. It would need a clearly defined local gap and strong replication to be more than a calibration exercise.

## 5. Competition value

A well-executed field study could be more engaging than a reanalysis of old data because it shows original fieldwork. But:

- the literature is crowded;
- one season gives weak inference;
- the result may be a local calibration curve;
- the timeline does not fit 2027;
- it does not test the original early-warning question.

It is not obviously stronger than a rigorous two-lake measurement audit, and it is clearly weaker for the original ambition.

## 7. Minimum design requirements if adopted later

If the field study is pursued in a future year, it should include:

1. A clearly stated local question and gap, not a generic “does PC track biovolume?” question.
2. Co-located sensor and water samples, with sample time and depth.
3. Replicated samples across a range of biomass and community composition.
4. Microscopy identification to genus/species and biovolume/biomass measurement.
5. Sensor calibration and maintenance records.
6. Environmental covariates (temperature, turbidity, DOM, light) to interpret interference.
7. Pre-specified analysis and reporting, with no claim of early warning.
8. A supervisor/taxonomist and documented permits.

---

## 8. Verdict

**Do not adopt the field study as the primary project.** It is scientifically coherent but incremental, resource-intensive, and chronologically incompatible with SciFest 2027. It may be a **Stage 1b add-on** only if fully resourced and tightly scoped, or a **future-year project**. It is not a substitute for Stage 1 or Stage 2.
